SPRINGFIELD, Va. (Nov. 8) Federal jobs, a $356 tax-bill increase, missing crossing guards and an underfunded school transfer — five days after an election that changed almost nothing about who represents them, Springfield's to-do list for its seven members of government is strikingly concrete.

Springfield is represented in the U.S. Senate by two Democrats, Mark Warner and Tim Kaine, and only one of those seats was on the ballot in this month's midterm elections.

Warner, seeking a fourth term, defeated Republican Bert Mizusawa, a retired Army major general and former deputy secretary of the Army, by about **61% to 39%** — a roughly 22-point margin — on Nov. 3, according to unofficial results. The 71-year-old senator has said this term will be his last. "This is my last campaign," Warner told supporters at an election night event in Alexandria, adding that he would spend the coming years pressing for a single-payer health insurance system, more affordable homeownership and new guardrails on artificial intelligence.

Kaine, Virginia's junior senator, was not on the ballot. He won a third term in 2024 and is not up for reelection until 2030.

Springfield's U.S. House representation was also on the ballot. Most of the community sits in Virginia's 11th Congressional District, where Rep. James Walkinshaw won a second term with about 74% of the vote over Republican Arthur Purves. A portion lies in the 8th District, where Rep. Don Beyer won a seventh term over Republican Tony Sabio.

Beyond the federal races, Springfield voters joined the rest of Virginia in approving three constitutional amendments, according to unofficial results: a right to reproductive health care, including abortion; the removal of now-unenforceable language barring same-sex marriage; and automatic restoration of voting rights for people who have completed felony sentences. The amendments take effect Jan. 1. Fairfax County voters also approved two bond questions — $180 million for parks and $75 million for community health and human services — each with about 70% support.

No Springfield-specific state or county offices were on the 2026 ballot. Virginia holds its General Assembly and local elections in odd-numbered years, and those seats are next up in 2027. Until then, Springfield is represented in Richmond by Sen. Dave Marsden (D-35th) and Del. Kathy Tran (D-18th). On the Fairfax County Board of Supervisors, Springfield District Supervisor Pat Herrity, a Republican, holds the seat and has said he will seek a sixth term next year. Sandy Anderson represents the Springfield District on the county School Board. Gov. Abigail Spanberger, a Democrat elected in 2025, took office in January.

## What residents actually ask for

There is no single survey titled "what Springfield wants." But the county's budget record, Herrity's district newsletters — which function as a running log of constituent complaints — federal workforce data and the Nov. 3 results add up to a short, concrete list.

**1. Protect the paycheck.** Fairfax County is home to roughly 80,000 federal employees and thousands more contractors, and the 2025 workforce reductions and shutdown hit it harder than almost anywhere else. County unemployment rose 35% year over year by May 2025, to its highest level since 2021; job openings fell from 48,000 in February to 26,800 in August. By March 2026, Brookings found Greater Washington leading the nation in regional job loss. For Warner, Kaine, Walkinshaw and Beyer, the asks are unglamorous: casework on fired employees' appeals, help for contractors outside civil-service protections, and no repeat of the shutdown.

**2. Hold the tax line while funding schools.** Fairfax's FY 2027 advertised budget holds the real estate tax rate at $1.1225 per $100 of assessed value — but the average homeowner's bill still rises about $356. It cuts $32.9 million and 107 merit positions, bringing four-year reductions to nearly $125 million, while adding $99.2 million to the school transfer. That still leaves the schools up to $44 million short of the superintendent's request. The message from budget town halls is a contradiction residents keep repeating: don't raise the bill, but don't cut the classroom.

**3. Fix the traffic and the school crossings.** Herrity's district has spent two years on Fairfax County Parkway and Roberts Road changes, Lee Chapel Road and Bauer Drive. The region re-took the title of worst traffic congestion in the country. And when the county eliminated high school crossing guards to save money, residents pushed back — loudly — after a student was struck near South Lakes High School; guards were temporarily reinstated at 11 locations. Springfield parents want them permanent.

**4. Make housing affordable without remaking the neighborhood.** This is the sharpest local split. Herrity titled one newsletter "A Big Step Backward for Affordable Housing" and another "Massive Homes in Quaint Neighborhoods"; residents packed a community meeting over subsidized housing at the Chantilly Library. Younger renters and the county's own housing staff want more supply; established homeowners want scale, parking and character respected. Both groups show up, and both are constituents.

**5. Keep the neighborhood safe and livable.** Repeat violent offenders, panhandling, the county's proposed trash takeover — the "trash police" fight drew one of the biggest town-hall turnouts in years — and restored curbside glass recycling all land on the supervisor's desk before they land anywhere else.

## The asks that changed the calendar

The three amendments are the clearest instruction Springfield voters gave anyone. Support ranged from 62% to 56% statewide, and the region ran ahead of that. Implementation now falls to Marsden and Tran in Richmond and to the governor's agencies — and to the 2027 General Assembly session, the next moment any of these officials face voters.



**The one-sentence version residents keep giving their representatives:** protect the paycheck, hold the tax bill, fix the road, and don't gamble with the schools.
